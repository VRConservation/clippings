# Annotated Bibliography: Landscape Conservation Forecasting

## Primary Sources (Base Links)

### 1. Provencher, L., Badik, K.J., Byer, S., Saito, L., Horner, M., Hamilton, B., Clifford, M., & McHugh, D. (2026). Landscape conservation forecasting to evaluate ecological condition and wildlife habitat suitability in eastern Nevada U.S.A. *Restoration Ecology*, e70445.

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1111/rec.70445](https://doi.org/10.1111/rec.70445)

This study applies state-and-transition simulation models (STSMs) to evaluate landscape conservation and restoration across 161,569 hectares in eastern Nevada. Researchers examined how vegetation treatments, prescribed fire, climate change, and management investments influence ecological condition, wildfire activity, and Rocky Mountain bighorn sheep habitat suitability over 50-year projections. Two management scenarios were crossed with three climate projections applied to 22 focal ecological systems. Key findings include that drier climate suppressed fire but increased precipitation variability raised fire and avalanche frequency; vegetation treatments decreased area burned at lower elevations where non-native annual fuels facilitated fires; ecological departure from reference conditions was unchanged when seedings included introduced species but decreased with native species seeding; and prescribed fire and increased avalanche frequency improved bighorn sheep habitat suitability. This paper demonstrates the practical application of Landscape Conservation Forecasting (LCF) for multi-objective land management planning.

### 2. Lawler, J.J., Wiersma, Y.F., & Huettmann, F. (2011). Using species distribution models for conservation planning and ecological forecasting. In C.A. Drew, Y.F. Wiersma, & F. Huettmann (Eds.), *Predictive Species and Habitat Modeling in Landscape Ecology: Concepts and Applications* (pp. 271–290). Springer, New York, NY.

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4419-7390-0_14](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4419-7390-0_14)

This book chapter reviews the application of species distribution models (SDMs) in two conservation contexts: planning and ecological forecasting. The authors describe how SDMs—correlative models linking species occurrences to environmental variables—can be used to identify priority conservation areas, evaluate habitat under future climate scenarios, and forecast shifts in species ranges. The chapter provides a foundation for understanding how predictive habitat modeling integrates with landscape-scale conservation strategies, including the state-and-transition simulation approaches used in Landscape Conservation Forecasting. It is frequently cited in subsequent work on conservation prioritization and climate-adaptive management.

**URL:** [https://www.conservationgateway.org/collections/land/landscape-conservation-forecasting-ecological-condition-wildlife-habitat-eastern-nevada/](https://www.conservationgateway.org/collections/land/landscape-conservation-forecasting-ecological-condition-wildlife-habitat-eastern-nevada/)

## Supporting Citations (Google Scholar)

### 4. Daniel, C.J., Frid, L., Sleeter, B.M., & Fortin, M.-J. (2016). State-and-transition simulation models: A framework for forecasting landscape change. *Methods in Ecology and Evolution*, 7(11), 1413–1423.

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12597](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12597)

This foundational paper presents the state-and-transition simulation model (STSM) framework as a general approach for forecasting landscape dynamics. STSMs divide a landscape into discrete spatial units and simulate the state of each cell forward as a discrete-time stochastic process, using an adapted Markov chain approach. The framework supports projections of vegetation change, land-use/land-cover change, and associated processes such as wildfire and management interventions. The accompanying ST-Sim software in the SyncroSim platform enables practitioners to construct and run STSMs. The paper demonstrates the method using a Hawai'i LULC model incorporating agriculture expansion, urbanization, wildfire, shrub encroachment, tree plantation harvest, and climate-driven moisture zone shifts. This work provides the theoretical and computational backbone for the LCF applications described in Provencher et al. (2021, 2026).

---

### 5. Provencher, L., Badik, K.J., Anderson, T., Tuhy, J., Fletcher, D.A., York, E., et al. (2021). Landscape conservation forecasting for data-poor at-risk species on western public lands, United States. *Climate*, 9(5), 79.

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.3390/cli9050079](https://doi.org/10.3390/cli9050079)

This paper applies the LCF methodology to the Black Mountains of southwestern Utah, where the Bureau of Land Management needed to inform vegetation management supporting greater sage-grouse and Utah prairie dog planning. Ecological systems were mapped from satellite imagery and modeled under two management levels (custodial and preferred) and three climate scenarios (historic and two GCM projections) for 60-year simulations. All ecological systems were at least moderately departed from reference conditions. Habitat suitability was 50.5% for sage-grouse and 48.4% for prairie dog. The drier climate most affected ecological departure and prairie dog habitat at 30 years. Management actions replaced non-native annual grasslands with perennial grasses, removed conifers, and controlled exotic forbs. This study extends LCF by incorporating habitat suitability indices as an additional metric beyond ecological departure.

---

### 6. Blankenship, K., Frid, L., & Smith, J.L. (2015). A state-and-transition simulation modeling approach for estimating the historical range of variability. *Science* 2, 253–268.

This paper describes how STSMs can be used to estimate the historical range of variability (HRV) for ecological systems—a critical benchmark for measuring ecological departure and guiding restoration targets. The approach runs reference-condition simulations to equilibrium and compares the resulting proportional distribution of vegetation classes to current conditions. This methodology underpins the ecological departure metric used throughout LCF projects, including the Provencher et al. (2021, 2026) studies.

---

### 7. Furlaud, J.M., Szetey, K., Williams, K.J., Provencher, L., Prober, S.M., & Richards, A.E. (2025). State-and-transition simulation models: How can we use them to assess ecosystem condition and support nature markets. *Methods in Ecology and Evolution*.

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.70122](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.70122)

This recent paper explores two approaches for using STSMs to assess ecosystem condition: (1) a "state-condition" approach that assigns static condition scores to states and simulates spatial-temporal variation, and (2) an "ecological similarity" approach comparing current proportions of vegetation classes to the historical range of variation in the reference state. The authors argue that STSMs are well-suited for monitoring and reporting on restoration outcomes required by emerging nature markets, because they explicitly link human management actions with ecological outcomes and can incorporate multiple knowledge systems.

---

### 8. Miller, B.W., & Frid, L. (2021). A new approach for representing agent-environment feedbacks: Coupled agent-based and state-and-transition simulation models. *Landscape Ecology*, 37(1), 43–58.

**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1007/s10980-021-01282-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10980-021-01282-y)

This paper introduces a method for coupling agent-based models (ABMs) with state-and-transition simulation models (STSMs) to represent feedbacks between land management decisions and ecological outcomes. By integrating agent-based representations of human behavior (e.g., fire management acceptance, budget allocation) with STSM vegetation dynamics, the coupled approach enables more realistic simulations of socio-ecological systems. This extension is relevant to LCF projects that need to model how management resistance, social acceptance, or institutional constraints interact with ecological processes.

---

### 9. The Nature Conservancy. (2026). *Landscape Conservation Forecasting Handbook*.

**URL:** [https://www.conservationgateway.org/collections/landscape-conservation-forecasting/handbook/](https://www.conservationgateway.org/collections/landscape-conservation-forecasting/handbook/)

This handbook explains the origins, purpose, and methodology of Landscape Conservation Forecasting. It describes the "3 Ms" of LCF—Maps, Models, and Metrics—and provides detailed guidance on team roles, area selection, building STSMs, basic and advanced modeling features, metrics of success, and report writing. The handbook documents the evolution of LCF from early LANDFIRE-inspired projects through the first complete implementation in the Bodie Hills (Low et al. 2010) and subsequent applications across Nevada, Utah, and California. It serves as the definitive reference for practitioners seeking to implement LCF methodology.

---

### 10. Provencher, L., Tuhy, J., et al. (2011). *Great Basin National Park Landscape Conservation Forecasting Report*. The Nature Conservancy.

**URL:** [https://www.conservationgateway.org/ConservationByGeography/NorthAmerica/UnitedStates/nevada/water/Documents/GBNP_LandscapeConservationForecasting_%20Report_2011-1-12.pdf](https://www.conservationgateway.org/ConservationByGeography/NorthAmerica/UnitedStates/nevada/water/Documents/GBNP_LandscapeConservationForecasting_%20Report_2011-1-12.pdf)

This technical report details the first LCF application at Great Basin National Park under a 2009 Cooperative Agreement between the Park and TNC (amended in 2010 to incorporate climate change). The process included remote sensing of satellite imagery, refinement of predictive state-and-transition ecological models for 22 focal biophysical settings, calculation of ecological departure scores using the Fire Regime Condition Class methodology, and simulation of minimum and preferred management scenarios over 50 years. Preferred management strategies reduced ecological departure for all ten focal biophysical settings and contained high-risk vegetation classes. Most treatments were front-loaded over three years with long-term benefits.

---

### 11. Provencher, L., Low, C., et al. (2009). Landscape Conservation Forecasting for the Bodie Hills, California and Nevada. The Nature Conservancy.

Referenced in the LCF Handbook as the first complete implementation of LCF combining local remote sensing, management STSMs, and ecological return-on-investment analysis. The Bodie Hills project established the template for subsequent LCF applications, including the use of TELSA (Tool for Exploratory Landscape Scale Analysis) for spatial simulation and an experimental factorial approach to test management scenario performance. This project demonstrated the feasibility of large-scale, stakeholder-driven landscape forecasting.

## Thematic Summary

The works in this bibliography span the development and application of Landscape Conservation Forecasting and its core methodology of state-and-transition simulation modeling. Key themes include:

- **Methodological foundations:** Daniel et al. (2016) establish the STSM framework; Blankenship et al. (2015) detail HRV estimation; Furlaud et al. (2025) extend STSMs to ecosystem condition assessment.
- **Applied forecasting:** Provencher et al. (2011) document early LCF at Great Basin National Park; Provencher et al. (2021) apply LCF to at-risk species in Utah; Provencher et al. (2026) extend LCF to bighorn sheep habitat and ecological condition in eastern Nevada.
- **Habitat modeling integration:** Lawler et al. (2011) review species distribution models as tools for conservation planning and ecological forecasting, providing the conceptual bridge between SDMs and the habitat suitability indices used in LCF.
- **Socio-ecological extensions:** Miller & Frid (2021) couple agent-based models with STSMs; the LCF Handbook documents the operational methodology for multi-stakeholder landscape planning.
